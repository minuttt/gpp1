1. What GP Paper 1 is actually testing

Core principle: GP is not an English essay competition disguised as current affairs. Knowledge supplies the material; the marks come from how intelligently you use it to answer the exact question.

Answer the question

Do not merely write everything you know about the topic. Identify the scope, command word, dominant keyword and hidden comparison or judgement.

Reason

Explain why your claim follows. A fact is not analysis. “X happened” is evidence; “this matters because…” is reasoning.

Evaluate

Ask how far the point holds, for whom, under what conditions, compared with what, and whether it is sufficient to prove the question’s claim.

Communicate

Maintain controlled academic expression, accurate grammar, clear paragraphing, logical connectors and precise vocabulary.

Cambridge examiner pattern: official examiner reports repeatedly emphasise focused introductions, sustained attention to the key words, coherent organisation, relevant examples, analysis and evaluation throughout, and conclusions that do more than repeat the essay. citeturn0search36turn0search37turn0search38turn0search39

2. Decode the question before brainstorming

Topic→Scope→Command→Keyword→Judgement

Scope

  • your society?
  • today?
  • developing countries?
  • all people?
  • the modern world?

Dominant keywords

Words such as only, always, no longer, realistic, necessary, harmful, effective, relevant, should, more than, entirely determine the burden of proof.

Command words

Discuss / Comment: examine competing views.
How far / To what extent: make a degree-of-agreement judgement.
Is it true / Is this fair: test the accuracy of the proposition.
Assess: weigh relative strength.

Never ignore absolutes. “No longer”, “only”, “all”, “always”, “completely”, “never” are not decoration. A strong essay tests whether the absolute wording survives exceptions, context and counter-cases.

Useful pre-writing paraphrase

Rewrite the question in one plain sentence. If you cannot explain what the examiner is asking in ordinary language, do not start the essay.

3. The 3–5 minute plan

Step 1 — Stand

Choose the position you can defend best, not the one that sounds most sophisticated.

Step 2 — Find 3 strong reasons

Prefer different dimensions rather than three versions of the same point: e.g. political + economic + social; immediate + long-term; individual + state + global.

Step 3 — Find the strongest opposing case

Do not invent a weak straw man. Ask: “What would a smart examiner say against my position?”

Step 4 — Decide whether rebuttal is logically required

If your stand is sweeping/absolute, an opposing argument can contradict it unless you deal with it. If your stand is genuinely qualified, do not mechanically rebut every opposing point.

Step 5 — Attach examples to arguments

Every example must have a job: prove the mechanism, demonstrate scale, reveal a limitation, or provide a counter-case.

Classic structure

Introduction→OV1 + rebuttal→OV2 + rebuttal→SV1→SV2→SV3→Conclusion

This is a useful template, not a rigid Cambridge requirement. Opposing views can appear later, and paragraph count is not itself a marking criterion.

4. Introduction: establish the debate, not a history lesson

Reliable formula

Hook → Link → Define/qualify → Interpret the claim → Stand

A hook can be a historical event, significant current development, concise observation, apt quotation, or well-written framing. Do not force a hook if it becomes decorative.

Definitions

Define terms that materially affect the debate. Avoid dictionary definitions. Give an operational definition: what will count as “relevant”, “harm”, “realistic”, “traditional media”, “success”, etc.?

Stand

Your final sentence should answer the question directly and show degree/context. A strong stand often has the shape: “Although X, I argue Y, particularly because A and B; however, this is less true where C.”

Do not list your body points mechanically. Cambridge specifically warns against introductions that become descriptive or tangential, and recent examiner guidance says to keep them focused rather than listing arguments. citeturn0search38turn0search37

5. The body paragraph: PEEL is not enough

A high-level GP paragraph is better understood as:

P PointM MechanismE ExampleA AnalysisE EvaluationL Link/Judgement

Point

Make a claim that directly answers the question.

Mechanism

Show the causal chain. Why does this point produce the outcome?

Example

Give a specific, accurate case. Avoid dumping several unrelated examples.

Analysis

Explain what the example demonstrates about the broader issue.

Evaluation

Test the strength: scale, duration, stakeholders, context, alternatives, unintended effects, reversibility and trade-offs.

Link

Return to the question's keyword and state why the argument matters to the overall judgement.

Example ≠ analysis. “Singapore introduced X” is description. “This matters because X changes the incentive structure faced by…” is analysis. “However, its effectiveness is limited because…” is evaluation.

6. The difference between analysis and evaluation

LevelWhat it sounds likeWhat it does
Description“The government introduced…”States what happened.
Explanation“This was intended to…”Explains purpose/mechanism.
Analysis“This means that… because…”Connects cause, effect and significance.
Evaluation“However, this is only convincing where…”Tests limits and relative strength.
Judgement“Thus, although X, Y matters more because…”Weighs the evidence against the question.

Evaluation lenses

Scale: How widespread or significant?
Depth: Does it change behaviour or merely attitudes?
Duration: Temporary or sustainable?
Context: Which countries, groups or institutions?
Stakeholders: Who gains, who loses?
Counterfactual: What would happen without it?
Alternatives: Is another factor more important?
Trade-off: Does solving one problem create another?
Reversibility: Can the harm/benefit be undone?

The strongest evaluation does not merely say “there are limitations”. It identifies the limitation and explains why it changes the answer.

7. Opposing arguments and rebuttal

First: build a real opposing argument

Represent the strongest plausible case against your stand. A weak opposing view makes the essay look rehearsed.

Then: rebut only when logically necessary

For a sweeping stand, failing to rebut a contradictory OV can leave the essay internally inconsistent. For a qualified/middle-ground stand, a concession may be better than rejecting the opposing side.

Four rebuttal moves

  1. Precisely: turn the OV into support for your stand.
  2. False premise: show the OV assumes something untrue or unsupported.
  3. Even if: accept the OV temporarily but show it does not overturn your conclusion.
  4. Limited applicability: show that the OV works only in particular contexts.
Critical warning: Do not use “some exceptions exist” to rebut an absolute claim you have agreed with. If one genuine exception exists, the absolute claim is already weakened. Either qualify your stand or show why the alleged exception does not actually count.

8. Conclusion: synthesis, not summary

Answer the question→Weigh arguments→Return to keyword→Final insight

A weak ending repeats three topic sentences. A stronger conclusion explains why the balance of arguments produces the judgement.

High-level ending

1. Restate the nuanced stand.
2. Identify which side is ultimately stronger and why.
3. Address the key qualifier in the question.
4. End with a broader implication or return to the opening uncertainty.

Cambridge examiner guidance explicitly values conclusions that evaluate and synthesise rather than merely repeat points; earlier examiner guidance also notes that linking the conclusion back to the introduction can create a sense of planning and sustained clarity. citeturn0search37turn0search39

9. Examples: build a flexible bank

Do not memorise essays. Memorise what examples prove. A strong example bank records: what happened → why it matters → what it supports → limitation/counterpoint.

Singapore

Use concrete local policy, institutions, demographic trends, education, housing, transport, media, sustainability and multiculturalism examples where relevant.

International

Use a smaller number of well-understood cases that demonstrate contrast across political systems, development levels or cultures.

Multi-use examples

One carefully understood case can support different questions when framed differently. The argument determines the use, not the other way around.

Accuracy

Never invent statistics, dates, quotations or causal claims. If uncertain, use a safer qualitative formulation rather than a false precision.

Example test

  1. Can I name the actors?
  2. Can I explain what actually happened?
  3. Can I connect it to my point?
  4. Can I say what it does not prove?

10. Core topic frameworks

Politics / Global Affairs

Power: hard vs soft power, legitimacy, sovereignty, deterrence.
Systems: democracy, populism, institutions.
Global: nationalism vs globalism, interdependence, diplomacy, intervention vs sovereignty.

Media

Credibility, gatekeeping, agenda-setting, misinformation, business models, censorship, freedom of expression, algorithms, citizen journalism, concentration of ownership.

Environment

Growth vs sustainability, collective action, externalities, individual vs systemic responsibility, developed vs developing states, incentives, enforcement, long-term costs.

Arts / Culture

Identity, heritage, criticism of society, creativity, emotional experience, education, tourism/revenue, accessibility, commercialisation.

Science / Technology

Productivity, innovation, inequality, safety, ethics, regulation, unintended consequences, human agency, access and concentration of power.

Education / Work

Economic productivity vs holistic development; employability vs citizenship; skills vs knowledge; automation; inequality of opportunity; purpose and wellbeing.

Economics / Corporations

Profit incentives, competition, consumer welfare, externalities, CSR, innovation, employment, inequality, regulation and market power.

Society / Equality

Equality vs equity, law vs culture, representation, structural barriers, individual responsibility, intersection of class, gender, age and opportunity.

12. Exam-room checklist

Before writing

  • Have I decoded the exact question?
  • What is the dominant keyword?
  • What is my stand?
  • What are my 3 strongest arguments?
  • What is the strongest OV?
  • Does my OV need rebuttal?

During writing

  • Does each paragraph answer the question?
  • Did I explain the mechanism?
  • Did I use a specific example?
  • Did I analyse rather than describe?
  • Did I evaluate the strength/limits?
  • Am I maintaining my stand?

Final check

  • Have I addressed every key word?
  • Is the conclusion a judgement?
  • Any factual overclaims?
  • Grammar, tense, agreement, punctuation?
  • Any repeated phrases or examples?
  • Is the handwriting legible?
The single best mental loop:
POINT → WHY? → EXAMPLE → SO WHAT? → HOW FAR? → THEREFORE?

Run this loop repeatedly. It forces the essay away from description and towards analysis, evaluation and judgement.

13. Model Paper 1 architecture: “Assess the view that traditional media platforms are no longer relevant.”

Stand: Traditional media has lost dominance, but “no longer relevant” is too absolute; its institutional credibility, agenda-setting and legitimacy remain significant, though unevenly and in adapted forms.

OV1 — Structural decline

Younger audiences, algorithmic platforms and advertising migration have weakened traditional media's audience share and economic position.

Rebuttal / evaluation

Loss of monopoly over attention is not identical to loss of societal relevance. Ask what kind of influence matters.

OV2 — Long-term structural threat

AI, citizen journalism, declining print readership and financial instability could further erode gatekeeping.

Rebuttal

Even if the physical formats decline, institutions can adapt distribution while retaining editorial functions. The question is whether institutional authority survives.

SV1 — Credibility

Professional standards, accountability and verification can become more valuable when misinformation proliferates.

SV2 — Agenda-setting / knowledge production

Investigative reporting can still generate stories that digital platforms amplify, debate and redistribute.

SV3 — Context / adaptation

Relevance differs by demographics, infrastructure and political context; hybrid digital models mean “traditional” institutions can remain influential even when print or broadcast formats decline.

Conclusion

Do not say simply “traditional media is important”. The stronger judgement is that relevance has shifted from monopoly over speed and distribution toward authority, verification and institutional legitimacy.
